BAGM: Learning Spatial Cell Representations in
Gastric Tissue
Revised draft · 17 October 2026 · Implementation and evidence reviewed through 12:49 UTC
Abstract
Spatial transcriptomics enables cell states to be studied within their tissue context. We introduce Bio-Architectural
Graph Modeling (BAGM), which learns cell representations by predicting randomly masked expression features from
visible RNA measurements, morphology/imaging covariates and spatial neighborhoods. Relative geometry contributes
an additive bias to scaled dot-product graph attention, and training minimizes Huber loss at masked entries.
Exploratory clustering analyses show changes in spatial partitions between initial and final cell representations;
reported visual correspondence with putative cancer regions awaits independent annotation and matched baseline
evaluation. A completed donor-separated validation experiment improved masked Huber loss and mean absolute error
over constant predictors, while mean squared error was slightly worse. A separate, ongoing paper-specific experiment
holds out cores while permitting donor overlap. These observations motivate controlled predictive and region-level
evaluation, followed by attention and sensitivity analyses of model-derived dependencies.
1. Introduction
Spatial transcriptomics connects molecular measurements with the organization of intact tissue. Platforms such as the
CosMx Spatial Molecular Imager support highly multiplexed RNA and protein profiling with subcellular localization
[1]. Here, BAGM uses RNA expression and selected morphology/imaging covariates from gastric tissue; it does not
jointly model a protein-expression assay.
Self-supervised learning provides representations without exhaustive cell annotations. Masked reconstruction is
established in computer vision [2], while scGPT uses generative pretraining for cellular expression data [3]. Spatial
methods incorporate tissue organization: GraphST combines graph-based contrastive learning with expression
reconstruction [4], and SpaMask [5] and the SAGE-FM preprint [6] use masked prediction in spatial representations.
These studies motivate learning from both molecular measurements and neighborhood structure.
An informative clustering representation does not itself establish molecular interactions. Predicting hidden expression
supplies a complementary task for examining conditional predictive dependencies. MISTy illustrates how information
from different spatial contexts can be used to investigate molecular associations [7]. BAGM similarly aims to support
tissue description and subsequent analysis of candidate dependencies, with biological interpretation requiring
additional evidence.
We examine whether BAGM representations delineate putative tumor-associated tissue domains and how those
representations change across graph layers. A node represents a cell, an edge a candidate spatial relationship, and an
edge feature measured relative geometry. These choices provide a biological inductive bias; they do not establish that
every edge is a direct cellular interaction. The present manuscript separates implemented methods, completed
exploratory observations and evaluation that remains pending.

2. Methods
Data and covariates. The analyzed cohort contains 407,659 mapped cells from 28 gastric tissue cores across two
CosMx slides and 14 donor groups. Inputs comprise 1,000 biological RNA genes and 22 morphology/imaging features.
Clinical labels, cell identifiers, slide identifiers, absolute coordinates and full-count library sizes are not node
covariates. Morphology preprocessing is fitted on training cells and then frozen.
Current paper-specific split. The fresh seed-0 experiment uses the whole-core partition below. Donor overlap is
permitted, both test cores are from the second slide, and these samples were examined in earlier experiments. This is a
holdout for the new fit, not new-donor validation or a historically untouched confirmatory test. Preprocessing is
refitted on the 21 training cores only; validation selects the checkpoint, and test evaluation follows checkpoint
selection.

Expression transformation and masking. We use standardized quasi-Poisson Pearson residuals, related to the
residual-normalization framework in [8], with dispersion factor 1.01 and an upper clip of 10 after standardization. This
is an expression transformation, not evidence of batch-effect removal. Each cell independently draws a number of
hidden genes uniformly from 0 to 1,000 inclusive; positions are sampled without replacement. There are ten training
views per core per epoch and three fixed evaluation views per core. Morphology remains visible. Each input view is
recomputed from visible raw counts and frozen training statistics so hidden counts cannot influence input
normalization. Fully observed transformed features are supervised targets only. The task predicts masked residual
features, not raw transcript counts.
Spatial graph and model. Coordinate-based graphs are constructed within each core by deterministic neighbor
selection within radial distance bands up to 500 μm, with a nominal 200-neighbor budget before bidirectional union.
There are no self-loops or cross-core edges. Each edge carries 70 relative-geometry features. These graphs include
broad neighborhood context and must not be equated with direct contact. A shared cell encoder combines visible
expression, mask indicators and morphology. Four independently parameterized graph Transformer blocks [9, 10]
update the cell states, and a decoder predicts expression features (Fig. 1; Appendix A).

2. Methods (continued)
Objective and evaluation. Huber loss with δ = 1 is averaged over masked entries within each view, then equally over
cores and views. AdamW uses learning rate 10−4, weight decay 10−5 and gradient-norm clipping at 1. The maximum is
300 epochs; early stopping uses equal-core validation masked Huber loss, a minimum of 50 epochs and patience of 25
validations without improvement exceeding 10−4. The best checkpoint tracks strict validation-loss improvements. Test
masks use a separate fixed namespace. Cells are correlated observations; donors are biological replication units, and
cores are the current holdout units.
Representation analysis. Leiden clustering [11] is applied to learned representations, including the initial state h(0)
and final state h(4). Fully observed embedding extraction is a separate analysis from masked prediction. PCA-based
representations provide a descriptive comparator. To claim improved cancer-region delineation, clustering settings
must be matched and maps scored against independently recorded region annotations.
3. Results and current status
Exploratory tissue representations. Completed analyses show that the initial and final representations yield different
spatial partitions. For the completed joint-slide model, fully observed embeddings from all 407,659 cells produced 16
initial-state and 22 final-state primary clusters, with adjusted Rand index 0.370 between the partitions. This joint
analysis includes training and validation cells. It establishes a change in representation, not tumor-boundary accuracy or
independent biological replication.
Preliminary visual inspection by the authors suggested clearer cancer-region correspondence after four layers and
relative to PCA. These observations remain qualitative. Independent clinician region masks and a matched quantitative
comparison were not available in the documented analyses. Existing PCA and embedding clustering pipelines also
differ in settings. Consequently, improved cancer delineation is not established, and differences between h(0) and h(4)
cannot be attributed specifically to geometry rather than attention, feed-forward transformations, residual updates or
smoothing.
Completed predecessor prediction experiment. A separate single-seed experiment used 20 training cores from ten
donor groups and eight validation cores from four other donor groups, with no independent test partition. Validation
cores had already been examined during preprocessing diagnostics, so the results remain exploratory. The
validation-selected model achieved equal-core masked Huber loss 0.196114, pooled mean absolute error 0.308160,
pooled mean squared error 1.005888 and pooled Pearson correlation 0.134499. Across three constant references,
Huber loss improved by 6.21–6.72% and mean absolute error by 26.8–28.2%, while mean squared error was
0.58–0.70% worse. These are mixed exploratory validation results; they do not establish graph-specific benefit. They
belong to the completed 20/8-core study, not the ongoing 21/5/2-core fit.
Pending paper-specific evaluation. At the review cutoff, the current 21/5/2-core experiment was still training; its
final test evaluation and test-only Leiden analysis were pending. Current Pearson-feature regression is evaluated using
regression metrics. Earlier nonzero-count detection diagnostics used other models and preprocessing; their precision,
recall or average precision must not be presented as results of this run. No quantitative cancer-region accuracy is
reported here.

4. Discussion and conclusion
BAGM provides an exploratory framework for learning spatial cell representations and predicting masked expression
features. The available evidence supports changes in representation and limited predictive improvement over constants.
Partial-gene prediction may exploit within-cell co-expression, so this gain alone does not establish a benefit from
neighborhood information. It does not yet establish improved cancer-region delineation, a graph-specific predictive
gain or a cellular mechanism. Larger datasets may improve precision of evaluation, but they do not replace appropriate
controls or independent biological replication.
Priorities are matched cell-autonomous, morphology, neighborhood-composition and spatial-smoothing baselines;
graph and geometry ablations; multiple training seeds; and independent region annotations. Region-level Dice/IoU and
boundary agreement require a predefined mapping from clusters to regions. Patient-held-out evaluation and uncertainty
across biological units are needed for new-patient generalization. Cell composition, broad tissue fields, core/slide
effects and segmentation spillover remain alternative explanations. Binary cross-entropy or hurdle formulations would
address different targets and require controlled comparisons; the present evidence does not identify Huber loss as the
cause of a detection deficit.
Attention describes normalized computational routing and is not, by itself, biological importance. Planned attribution
and Jacobian analyses will quantify sensitivity of selected predicted features to visible transformed input features, with
morphology, masks and graphs held fixed. Raw-count derivatives are different because they include the
exposure-normalization pathway. One-hop effects must be distinguished from multihop dependencies. Bounded input
perturbations and message ablations assess faithfulness; separate tests assess stability and calibration against realistic
nulls. Independent molecular evidence and controlled perturbation are needed to support mechanism and causality,
respectively. Biological interpretation should focus on targets with reproducible graph-specific predictive gain.
Appendix A. Current graph attention architecture
Cell encoder. Separate linear projections of the visible expression vector, mask and 22 morphology/imaging covariates
are summed with a learned bias to form a 256-dimensional state, followed by LayerNorm, GELU and dropout. Hidden
expression entries are zero. Four independently parameterized pre-normalized graph blocks each use eight heads of
dimension 32.

The geometry output weights and biases start at zero. The current model uses queries and keys without additional L2
normalization, with fixed 1/√32 scaling; it has no geometry feature gate or learned attention temperature. Geometry
directly enters logits only. Queries, keys and values are recomputed at each layer. The incoming-neighbor softmax has
no self-edge coefficient; same-cell information is retained by residual paths.

residual addition. A second pre-normalized 256 → 1,024 → 256 GELU feed-forward transformation has another
residual connection. The 256 → 1,024 → 1,000 decoder uses GELU and dropout, with unconstrained outputs. Dropout

Review outcome
The supplied paper agrees with the broad research aim, but not fully with the current implementation or evidence. The
revised manuscript describes the current original Relative-QKV architecture, separates three distinct experiment
contexts, and calibrates biological claims. This was a review of code, protocols and existing aggregate results; no model
was retrained or new scientific result generated.

and redrew the schematic to match the active implementation. [S1]
